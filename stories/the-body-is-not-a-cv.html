The Invisible Jury

The human résumé and the burden of being constantly assessed.

For a long time, I felt as though I were living under observation.

As though an invisible committee had been appointed to assess every detail of who I was.

My body.

My age.

The way I dressed.

My work.

My home.

My money.

My personality.

Even the person standing beside me.

There were no judges, minutes or official verdicts.

And yet the judgement kept arriving.

Too fat.

Not muscular enough.

Too old.

Too emotional.

Too available.

Lazy.

Rich.

Poor.

Someone to take advantage of.

Someone who had not achieved enough.

Someone who had too much and therefore, presumably, did not deserve sympathy.

The curious thing is that you can be accused of everything and its opposite at the same time.

You are too confident.

You lack self-esteem.

You show too much emotion.

You are distant.

You take yourself too seriously.

You have no ambition.

Once someone has decided to judge you, consistency becomes a minor detail.

For years, I thought the problem was my body.

The body enters a room before our words do.

It is seen, measured and compared.

Sometimes it takes only a few seconds.

A photograph.

A profile on an app.

A shirt that falls in the wrong way.

A stomach that fails to meet expectations.

A face beginning to show its age, as though growing older were a lack of discipline rather than the fairly inevitable consequence of remaining alive.

I know that feeling well.

Not being slim enough.

Not being athletic enough.

Not having the kind of body that, in certain circles, seems to function as a form of identification.

The right body opens doors.

The wrong one is expected to explain itself first.

Over time, however, I realised that the body was only the first page.

Behind it lay an entire résumé.

Age.

Height.

Profession.

Income.

Postcode.

Car.

Homeowner or tenant.

Number of followers.

Relationship history.

Health status.

Perceived masculinity.

The ability to appear happy, independent and, preferably, entirely free from emotional needs.

We were no longer meeting a person.

We were reviewing an application.

And the saddest part was that, quite often, we were not looking for someone capable of loving us.

We were looking for someone who would improve our image.

Someone to introduce.

To photograph.

To take to dinner without worrying that they might lower the perceived value of our life.

As though a partner were an extension of our wardrobe.

As though entering a room with someone who was not young, beautiful, wealthy or desirable enough might damage the character we had created.

All of us minor celebrities.

All of us managers of our own image.

All of us terrified that someone might discover that, behind the display window, we are perfectly ordinary human beings.

Dating apps made this mechanism more visible, but they did not invent it.

They simply organised the jury more efficiently.

They turned desire into a sequence of photographs.

Complexity into a few lines of biography.

Human presence into a list of negotiable features.

Tall.

Masculine.

Muscular.

Discreet.

Successful.

No drama.

That final requirement has always amused me.

The people who announce that they want no drama are often the same people who have left an entire television series behind them.

Preferences, of course, exist.

Desire cannot be imposed, and we do not have to feel attracted to everyone in order to prove that we are good people.

The problem begins when a preference becomes a moral ranking.

When being muscular no longer means simply having a particular body, but being regarded as more disciplined, more desirable and somehow more worthy.

When being overweight is not treated as a physical characteristic, but as evidence of laziness, failure or a lack of control.

When age is no longer accumulated experience, but a decline in market value.

The body then becomes a résumé on which other people believe they can read things that were never written there.

They see a stomach and imagine a personality.

They see a wrinkle and declare desire officially over.

They see someone with money and assume he is willing to pay.

They see generosity and mistake it for an absence of boundaries.

They see vulnerability and interpret it as an invitation.

I have judged people too, of course.

It would be very convenient to write this article while pretending that I had always stood on the right side of the lens.

I have not.

I too have dismissed people too quickly.

I too have attached meaning to a coat, a body or a way of speaking.

I too have sometimes chosen image over curiosity.

The difference is that now I try, at least, to notice when I am doing it.

Because a moment always arrives when the person judging becomes the person being judged.

There will always be someone younger.

More beautiful.

Slimmer.

Richer.

More confident.

More photogenic.

The ranking never ends.

And anyone who builds their value through comparison is condemned to wait for the person who will make them feel inferior.

For years, I wondered which part of myself I needed to correct.

My body?

My clothes?

My emotions?

My generosity?

Perhaps I should have been less available.

Harder.

More strategic.

More capable of that Neapolitan quality known as cazzimma. It does not simply mean cruelty. It is the instinct not to allow other people to turn your kindness into a free service.

Then I understood that I could have changed everything and the jury would still have found another flaw.

The problem was not that I was imperfect.

The problem was the very idea that a person should have to pass an audition in order to deserve love, attention or respect.

I never wanted to be chosen because I completed a photograph nicely.

I wanted to be chosen because, after getting to know me, someone still wanted to stay.

With the body I had.

With the years.

With my habit of showing emotion rather than preserving it under vacuum.

With my generosity, even when it could have done with a few more gates around it.

With the enthusiasm, fears and contradictions.

With everything that would never fit neatly into a polished profile.

The interesting thing is that the very qualities that made me less desirable in some people’s eyes were the ones for which others came to love me.

The customers at my wine bar did not return because I had the perfect body.

They returned because they felt welcome.

My friends did not open their homes to me because I was flawless.

They did it because there was something real between us.

People did not trust me with their confessions because I looked like a minor celebrity.

They did it because they felt I would listen without turning their vulnerability into a performance.

None of this means that inner qualities make the body irrelevant.

The body matters.

It is desire.

Pleasure.

Health.

Memory.

Identity.

It has the right to be seen.

But it should not be forced to represent the whole person on its own.

A body is not a confession.

It does not automatically reveal how much we have loved, suffered, worked or endured.

It does not tell you whether we will be kind.

It does not guarantee loyalty.

It does not measure intelligence.

And it certainly does not reveal whether we are capable of remaining beside someone when the photograph stops looking perfect.

Perhaps the problem is not wanting to be beautiful.

It is believing that beauty makes us better.

It is not wanting to be seen.

It is using other people to support the image we have created of ourselves.

It is not having standards.

It is forgetting that, on the other side of those standards, there is a person who did not come into the world to be approved by us.

Today, I no longer try to persuade the jury.

Not because I have become completely immune to the opinions of others.

That would be an elegant lie, and I have seen enough elegant lies already.

There are still days when I look at myself and see what is missing before I see anything else.

There are photographs I do not like.

Rejections that hurt.

Moments when I feel under examination again.

But I try to remember that the invisible jury has no real authority.

It is made up of people who are equally frightened, equally imperfect and often far too busy hiding their own résumés to read anyone else’s properly.

The real luxury today is not having someone beside us whom everyone else envies.

It is having someone beside whom we do not have to audition.

Someone who does not ask us to be younger, slimmer, richer or less emotional so that we do not damage their image.

Someone who does not read our body as a verdict.

Someone who, after seeing the résumé, is still curious about the person.

And perhaps we should begin with ourselves.

Stop looking at ourselves as unfinished projects.

Stop living as though we were waiting for the presentable version of our life to begin.

The body changes.

Money comes and goes.

Success is far less stable than it likes to pretend.

Our image grows older with us.

In the end, what remains is how we made people feel.

Our presence.

Our ability to listen.

What we built.

The doors we opened.

And the freedom of having been ourselves, even when our résumé did not seem interesting enough.
