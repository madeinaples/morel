The Avoidable Return

Not every return is a second chance. Sometimes it is simply a rerun with better lighting.

It is almost eleven.

It has just stopped raining. The phone is face down.

Then it lights up.

A name.

Some names do not need an introduction. They arrive already carrying the room they used to live in.

You read the message and, for a moment, memory becomes a very persuasive estate agent.

Everything looks larger. Warmer. Better positioned.

Nostalgia does not always lie. But it edits beautifully.

That is what makes returns dangerous.

Not because the past was necessarily bad.

Sometimes it was good. Sometimes very good.

But memory has a habit of keeping the light and cutting the waiting, the confusion, the disappointment and all the small reasons we eventually walked away.

The second-chance story

We like second chances.

They are emotionally efficient.

They allow us to believe that the ending was merely an interruption.

That perhaps everybody has changed just enough for the same story to deserve another season.

Sometimes that is true.

People change. Circumstances change. We change.

But one question matters:

If everything is identical, it is not a second chance. It is a rerun.

The difficulty is that familiarity can feel like evidence.

We recognise the voice, the humour, the way someone writes our name.

The body remembers before the mind has completed the risk assessment.

The duty to give another chance

There is a moral glamour around reopening doors.

Forgive. Try again. Be mature. Do not hold grudges.

All useful ideas.

Until they become instructions.

The duty to give another chance.
The right to protect the distance we worked hard to create.

Distance is not always punishment.

Sometimes it is the result of understanding.

Sometimes we needed months or years to stop confusing intensity with compatibility, nostalgia with love, chemistry with safety.

Reopening a door can feel generous.

It can also undo work nobody else saw us doing.

What exactly has changed?

A return deserves more than a beautiful message.

It deserves evidence of difference.

Not promises. Not the mood created by rain and a familiar name at 10:53 p.m.

Something real.

Has the dynamic changed?

Has the person changed?

Have I?

Or have we simply been apart long enough for memory to become flattering?

I do not think every closed door should remain closed forever.

That would turn protection into another prison.

But neither do I think every knock deserves an answer.

Sometimes growth is not proving we can return without getting hurt.

Sometimes growth is realising we no longer need to test the door.

The phone was still there.

The name was still there.

The past had not become evil overnight.

It had simply become past.
