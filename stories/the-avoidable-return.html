The Avoidable Return

Why the past has the terrible habit of presenting itself as a second chance.

It is almost eleven.

Outside, the rain has just stopped.

The window reflects the room more than it reveals the street. A glass has been sitting untouched on the table for an hour, the television is talking to itself, and the phone lies face down.

Then it lights up.

It does not ring.

It does not even vibrate.

It simply shows a name.

One of those names you had eventually trained yourself not to look for anymore.

Two seconds pass.

Maybe three.

You do not touch the screen.

You only look at it.

The message is short.

“How are you?”

Three words.

No declaration.

No thunderstorm.

No violins.

Just three words innocent enough to look accidental.

And yet something has already entered the room.

The past.

The past rarely comes back dressed as the past.

If it did, it would be much easier to recognise.

It does not arrive carrying the worst photographs.

It does not remind you of the nights when you could not sleep.

It does not replay the phone call after which you decided it was over.

It does not show you the Monday morning when that job suddenly felt incompatible with being alive.

It does not mention the exact moment you bought a one-way ticket.

The past has learned marketing.

It returns restored.

The lighting is better.

So is the soundtrack.

And, mysteriously, a few details have disappeared in the edit.

With time, memories lose contrast.

Arguments become shorter.

Waiting becomes less humiliating.

The worst days acquire the strange patina of old photographs.

What remains is a dinner.

A laugh.

A street.

The bar on the corner.

A hand on your back.

September light on the front of a house.

It is remarkable how good the brain is at restoring buildings we probably should not live in again.

It removes the damp.

Hides the cracks.

Repaints the front door.

And when it has finished, it almost seems to ask:

You really left this place?

Then there is one advantage the past has over the future.

Familiarity.

The future is a dark room.

You do not know where the light switch is.

The past already knows where the furniture is.

You know how to walk through it.

You know where it will hurt.

But at least you know the exact spot.

Sometimes a familiar pain can feel oddly safer than a possibility that does not yet have a name.

A love.

A job.

A city.

Even a mistake.

The familiar has an unfair advantage:

it already knows how to talk to us.

When love comes back

This is probably the most recognisable version.

A relationship ends.

Badly, perhaps.

Or worse: politely.

With mature sentences.

With the kind of civilised calm that somehow makes everything feel even more final.

Months pass.

Maybe years.

Then one evening:

“I thought of you.”

And the brain immediately opens a department devoted to reviewing the case.

Maybe he has changed.

Maybe I have changed.

Maybe the timing was wrong.

Maybe we were afraid.

Maybe now...

Maybe is a powerful substance.

In small doses it is hope.

In large doses it alters the facts.

Because the question should not only be:

Do I miss him?

It should also be:

What has changed?

If that person lied, do they still lie?

If they disappeared, do they still disappear?

If they could not communicate, can they now?

If they wanted a life completely incompatible with yours, is anything actually different?

Not what you hope is different.

What is.

When we were the ones who left

This return is subtler.

It has a lower voice.

Almost reasonable.

“What if I was wrong?”

The question knows how to enter.

It does not force the door.

It sits down.

It waits.

You had made a difficult decision.

You had thought about it for weeks.

Perhaps months.

You had collected evidence.

Endured silences.

Postponed.

Justified.

Then one day you decided.

And time, which is an excellent defence lawyer for the past, slowly starts dismantling the case.

Maybe you were too harsh.

Maybe too impulsive.

Maybe you misunderstood.

Maybe you should have waited.

And slowly you forget something important:

you did not leave because of one moment.

You left because of a hundred small moments that, together, had finally stopped looking small.

When they were the ones who left

This return can be more dangerous.

Because we are not only looking for that person.

Sometimes we are looking for a correction.

A different verdict.

If they come back, perhaps we had value after all.

If they change their mind, perhaps we were not really rejected.

If it works this time, perhaps the first ending can somehow be erased.

But the past is not a document open on a computer.

You cannot highlight the final page and press Delete.

You can only add another one.

And before you do, it may be worth asking:

Do I really want this person?

Or:

Do I want this person to correct what their rejection made me think about myself?

Those are two very different desires.

Old jobs come back too

Sometimes by phone.

A familiar voice.

“Would you consider coming back?”

When you left, you remembered exactly why.

Sunday evenings ruined by Monday morning.

The endless meetings.

The boss.

The strange feeling that every day you left a small part of yourself in the lift.

Then two years pass.

And suddenly you remember the salary.

The funny colleague.

The certainty.

The café downstairs.

The commute you could do almost with your eyes closed.

The brain performs an extraordinary edit.

The worst scenes are left on the cutting-room floor.

The old job returns remastered in 4K.

Pity the script may be exactly the same.

And then there are cities

Cities are more elegant.

They do not text.

They do not call.

They wait.

You return after years.

You get off the train.

The station has been renovated but the air smells the same.

A street seems narrower than you remember.

One shop has gone.

Another, improbably, is still there.

It is raining.

Naturally.

You walk under an arcade or along a shining pavement and suddenly a lit window, a bus stop, even a sound can hand an entire life back to you.

For a moment you think:

I could live here again.

Perhaps you could.

But before opening Rightmove, Idealista or any other modern form of property-based delusion, there is a useful question:

Do I miss this city?

Or:

Do I miss the person I was when I lived here?

They are not the same thing.

Sometimes we do not want to return to a place.

We want to return to an age.

To a group of friends.

To a body.

To a home.

To that period when some possibilities had not yet closed.

Unfortunately, no estate agent rents flats in 1998.

Going back is not always a mistake

This matters.

I do not believe in the rule that says we should never go back.

It is a very elegant sentence.

And often a false one.

People change.

Cities change.

Jobs change.

We change.

Real second chances exist.

Some relationships work better the second time.

Some jobs become sustainable because the conditions have changed.

Some cities reveal themselves differently because we are different.

Sometimes going back simply means recognising that a decision made then no longer belongs to the person we are now.

But something must have changed.

Because if everything is identical, it is not a second chance.

It is a rerun.

Before opening the door

I am not particularly fond of lists that promise to solve life in seven steps.

But before a return, I would keep three questions.

Why did I leave?

Not the reason softened by time.

The real one.

What has actually changed?

Not feelings.

Not promises.

Facts.

And finally:

Do I really miss what I want to return to, or do I miss the person I was when it was part of my life?

That may be the most uncomfortable question.

Which is probably why it is worth asking.

And if we open anyway?

It happens.

We open.

We go back.

And sometimes discover that nothing had changed.

The relationship ends at the same place.

The job starts consuming us again.

The city begins to feel too small.

And then a voice arrives that is much less forgiving than the past.

“You knew.”

Then:

“You never learn.”

And perhaps the worst one:

“You let yourself get fooled again.”

This is the part I would watch carefully.

Because a bad decision can easily become a definition of who we are.

And it is not.

Going back does not mean we are weak.

It may mean we hoped.

That we wanted to check.

That we needed to know.

The problem is not making the same mistake twice.

The problem begins when, even after the second piece of evidence, we still insist the case should remain open.

At that point we are no longer giving the past a second chance.

We are taking one away from ourselves.

Some places deserve a second visit.

Some people deserve a second chance.

Some decisions can be corrected.

Perhaps the problem is not going back.

Perhaps it is knowing why we are going back.

Before crossing that doorway again, it may be worth standing on the threshold for a moment.

Looking inside.

And asking:

Am I going back because something has changed?

Or because time has blurred the reason I left?

Outside, it may have started raining again.

The phone is still on the table.

The name is still there.

The past knows the way home perfectly well.

And every now and then it has the nerve to ring the bell.

The fact that it knows where we live, however,

does not mean we have to open the door.
