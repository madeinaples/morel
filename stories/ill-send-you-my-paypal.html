I’ll Send You My PayPal

When seduction arrives with an invoice attached.

Five minutes ago, a forty-one-year-old man messaged me on an app:

“I’ll send you my PayPal if you want to watch me naked on the bed while I touch myself.”

Not “hello”.

Not “how are you?”

Not “what are you looking for on here?”

No social foreplay. No attempt to sound interesting. Not even one of those pointless questions people ask on apps while pretending they want to get to know you before requesting photographs you would not show your own dermatologist.

Straight to PayPal.

For a few seconds, I wondered whether I had misunderstood.

Perhaps, after making the payment, I would not simply be watching a naked man on a bed.

Perhaps the offer involved something truly extraordinary: an elephant marching behind the village brass band, playing the trombone with its trunk and keeping perfect time.

That, at least, would have had some rarity value.

But a naked man touching himself?

As far as I am aware, the internet already has a fairly substantial collection.

Many of them are even available free of charge, in high definition and with better lighting.

I have nothing against people who sell erotic content.

And I have nothing against those who choose to buy it.

Two consenting adults can do whatever they like with their bodies, their money and their time. The world is already crowded enough with people who believe they should supervise everybody else’s pleasures.

What struck me was not the financial proposition.

It was the certainty with which it was presented.

As though it were perfectly obvious that I would desire his body enough to pay to watch it.

As though every previous stage of conversation had become unnecessary.

He was naked.

I had PayPal.

From his point of view, the system was complete.

Perhaps he had made the same offer many times before.

Perhaps someone had accepted.

Perhaps this was simply how he earned a little money, without drama and without any particular romantic illusion.

Or perhaps he was looking for money and approval at the same time, a very contemporary combination: pay me, so I will know that I have value.

I cannot know.

What I do know is what the offer triggered in me.

Why would I pay?

Not “why would anyone pay?”

Someone else might have their reasons, their fantasies and their own relationship with money and desire.

But why would I?

What exactly had I been offered, apart from the seller’s confidence that the product must be irresistible?

I did not know his voice.

I did not know how he laughed.

We had not exchanged a thought, a joke or a confidence.

I had no idea whether he was intelligent, funny, kind or unbearable.

All I knew was that he was forty-one, owned a bed and had a PayPal account.

I have seen corporate presentations delivered with less confidence.

Perhaps age was the point.

He was forty-one.

I was sixty-one.

Perhaps, in his mind, those twenty years automatically increased the commercial value of his nudity.

Perhaps he imagined a sixty-year-old man as a creature stranded at the edge of the market, grateful for any attention from somebody younger.

A kind of pensioner of desire, ready to reach for his credit card at the first vaguely visible abdominal muscle.

It is a curious idea.

Especially because forty-one is not exactly adolescence.

He was not a young Renaissance prince who had just stepped down from a fresco.

He was an adult man offering another adult man a performance in front of a camera.

Legitimate.

But not miraculous.

The problem with part of the app world is that attention is often treated as currency.

When you are younger, more muscular or closer to a certain beauty ideal, you can begin to believe that other people are not simply looking at you.

They are applying for access.

And every form of access can carry a price.

A photograph.

A compliment.

A top-up.

A gift.

A PayPal payment.

Desire becomes a small private economy.

You own the body.

Other people own the need.

And between the two appears a button marked “Send money”.

Seduction once required at least a little choreography.

A look.

A sentence.

Some ability to create anticipation.

Now it risks becoming an administrative procedure.

Name.

Age.

Photograph.

Payment method.

Soon we will receive the invoice before the good morning.

Of course, desire itself has changed.

We live in an age when seeing a body is not difficult.

Bodies are everywhere.

On social media.

On apps.

In videos.

In advertisements.

In photographs that arrive without being requested and which, occasionally, we would rather not receive over breakfast.

Nudity is no longer rare.

Presence is.

A conversation that does not feel like an automated procedure.

Someone capable of creating curiosity before displaying the product.

Feeling chosen as a person rather than identified as a potential customer.

Perhaps that is why the offer did not shock me.

It bored me.

It was too easy.

Too direct.

Too convinced that seeing would be enough.

But seeing is not always desiring.

And desiring does not necessarily mean wanting to possess, purchase or finance.

Sometimes a body can be beautiful and leave us uninterested.

It can be available without feeling valuable.

It can be naked and, paradoxically, reveal absolutely nothing.

Because what makes someone erotic is not only what they uncover.

It is what they allow you to imagine.

A voice.

An intelligence.

A way of looking.

A vulnerability that appears for half a second before being hidden again.

A body may attract attention.

But it is the person who gives that attention a reason to remain.

Perhaps that man simply needed money.

Perhaps he collected customers with the same calm efficiency other people use to sell old clothes or furniture on Marketplace.

Perhaps, instead, he needed to feel desired and had found a way of turning every confirmation into a receipt.

I do not judge him for trying.

I judge only the implied idea that I should have considered his offer a privilege.

Not everything for sale has value to the person looking at it.

And not everything desirable automatically deserves payment.

I said no.

Without outrage.

Without a lecture.

Without attempting to explain the global economics of free pornography.

I simply thought that, to convince me, he would have needed to offer something rarer: the elephant behind the village band, the trombone in its trunk and an impeccable sense of rhythm.

That, I admit, I might have considered.
