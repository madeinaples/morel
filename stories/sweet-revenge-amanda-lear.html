Sweet Revenge: Amanda Lear Had Already Seen Our Future

A 1978 disco fable and a question that still feels current.

In 1978, the Devil did not have Instagram.

He knew nothing about algorithms, could not promise a million followers, and nobody had yet turned the word engagement into something capable of ruining your breakfast.

He had to work with more traditional tools.

Fame, for instance.

And a soul.

That is roughly where Sweet Revenge, Amanda Lear’s second album, released in 1978, begins.

For anyone who was not there — and for anyone who knows Amanda Lear only as a vaguely mythical figure who occasionally appeared on television — one thing is worth explaining.

Sweet Revenge is not simply a collection of disco songs.

The first side of the record is a continuous suite: Follow Me, Gold, Mother, Look What They’ve Done to Me, Run Baby Run and Follow Me (Reprise) form a small story inspired by the Faust myth. A woman gives in to the Devil’s temptation, seduced by the promise of fame and wealth; she discovers the price of the bargain, runs away and reaches a new kind of awareness.

All of this, naturally, while remaining perfectly danceable.

Faust under a mirror ball.

And that is where the record becomes interesting.

Not because of nostalgia.

Because almost fifty years later, we have changed the contract.

Not necessarily the desire.

The Amanda character

Amanda Lear has always interested me less as simply a singer than as an artist-character.

The voice.

The body.

The clothes.

The accent.

The theatre.

The contradictions.

And above all, that rare ability to let other people wonder about her without showing any particular urgency to provide a final answer.

For years the public tried to establish who Amanda Lear was.

Meanwhile, she simply continued being Amanda Lear.

It strikes me as the more intelligent strategy.

She understood that ambiguity can become a language.

That an image, when controlled rather than endured, can become artistic material.

And that sometimes mystery is not about hiding.

It is about preventing other people from convincing themselves they have understood you completely.

Not because Amanda Lear predicted TikTok.

Let us not get carried away.

But she had already touched something we know extremely well.

The need to be seen.

Follow Me

At the beginning there is a voice.

It does not command.

It invites.

Follow Me.

That is how the best temptations work.

They do not ask us to want something new.

They promise exactly what we already wanted.

To be admired.

To be recognised.

To be chosen.

To finally enter the room where we always imagined everyone else had already arrived.

The woman follows.

The bargain begins.

And it is difficult to judge her.

Because, remove the Devil and add Wi-Fi, and we are not so different.

Almost all of us want to be seen.

Sometimes by thousands of people.

Sometimes by one.

Desire is not the problem.

The question comes later.

How much are we prepared to change to keep that gaze on us?

Gold

Then comes the reward.

Gold.

Or at least what should be the reward.

Success shines.

It has to.

Otherwise it would be considerably harder to sell.

Fame.

Money.

Desirability.

The small privilege of entering a room knowing someone already knows your name.

In 1978 you needed record companies, radio, television, photographers and newspapers.

Today the system is far more democratic.

And far more efficient.

We can know exactly how many people watched us.

For how long.

From which country.

We can even discover that at 2:43 in the morning somebody in Belgium decided our video did not deserve its final seven seconds.

Progress is extraordinary.

The Devil now provides analytics.

The problem with numbers is that they look objective.

One hundred thousand people watch you.

One thousand.

Ten.

Nobody.

And it becomes very easy to make one small logical leap:

if attention falls, perhaps I am worth less.

Of course that is not true.

But some lies become remarkably persuasive when they come with a dashboard.

Look what they’ve done to me

Then comes the moment I find most interesting.

Mother, Look What They’ve Done to Me.

The transformation has happened.

And suddenly the woman can see it.

Look what I have become.

But who transformed us?

The industry?

The audience?

The platforms?

Other people?

Probably all of them, a little.

The more uncomfortable answer is that sometimes we collaborated.

We saw which version of ourselves worked.

The photograph that received more attention.

The body.

The joke.

The provocation.

The opinion.

Even vulnerability.

Even pain.

And we learned.

That photograph again.

That joke again.

That version of ourselves again.

One small adjustment after another.

Until what we built in order to be seen risks becoming what we must continue to be in order not to disappear.

This is where the Amanda Lear character becomes, to me, more interesting than Amanda Lear the celebrity.

Because building a character does not necessarily mean losing yourself.

It can mean knowing there is a distance between what you show and what you are.

And protecting that distance.

A character should be something you wear.
Not something you hand the keys to your house.

Run Baby Run

At some point, you have to leave.

Run Baby Run.

Not fame.

Not success.

Not visibility.

Turning this into a sermon against social media would be too easy.

And publishing such a sermon on the internet would also be slightly ridiculous.

We write because we want someone to read.

We sing because we want someone to listen.

We take photographs because sometimes we want to show what we have seen.

Creating also means leaving your own room and knocking on someone else’s.

The gaze is not the problem.

The problem begins when we live for the gaze.

When something beautiful is no longer enough simply to be beautiful.

It has to work.

A photograph has to perform.

A thought has to generate reactions.

A person has to become recognisable.

An identity has to become a brand.

And today we do not even need somebody else to build the character for us.

We can do it perfectly well ourselves.

Phone.

Light.

Account.

Publish.

Wait.

Check.

Something works.

Repeat it.

Something does not.

Change it.

Again.

At some point it becomes difficult to know whether we are using a platform or whether the platform is slowly teaching us which person it is more profitable to be.

The Devil changed the offer

If the Devil of Sweet Revenge worked today, I doubt he would simply promise:

I’ll make you famous.

It sounds almost old-fashioned.

Today he would say:

I’ll make you visible.

Much more elegant.

Almost reasonable.

Because fame can sound like vanity.

Visibility sounds like necessity.

If nobody sees what we do, does it exist?

If nobody reacts to what we say, does it matter?

If a wonderful dinner appeared nowhere online, are we sure we actually ate it?

Yes.

Of course we did.

But the fact that somebody may have hesitated for a second is interesting enough.

The contemporary bargain no longer requires a signature in blood.

Just click Accept.

Majors.

Platforms.

Algorithms.

Social media.

Personal branding.

The creator economy.

The intermediaries change.

The promise remains strangely familiar.

Follow me and you will become somebody.

And yet perhaps this is the oldest trick of all.

We were already somebody before the promise arrived.

Follow Me Reprise

Then the voice returns.

This, to me, is the most beautiful detail.

Follow Me (Reprise).

The theme that began everything comes back at the end of the suite.

The temptation has not disappeared.

And that is exactly right.

It would be a much less interesting story if understanding something once made us immune forever.

Success remains seductive.

Beauty remains seductive.

Recognition remains seductive.

Being desired remains pleasant.

Being applauded does too.

There is no need to pretend otherwise in order to feel morally superior.

But something has changed.

The first time the voice says:

Follow me.

And we hear only the promise.

When it returns, we also know the price.

We may still follow.

But it is no longer the same choice.

Perhaps that is the most modern part of Sweet Revenge.

The revenge is not necessarily defeating the Devil.

It is not suddenly becoming pure, wise and immune to desire.

How boring.

The revenge is subtler.

Understanding the game without having to stop playing.

Amanda Lear put all of this on a record in 1978.

A woman.

A temptation.

Fame.

Transformation.

Escape.

And a voice that returns at the end.

We added smartphones, platforms, algorithms and a frankly excessive number of passwords.

But we still want very similar things.

To be seen.

Chosen.

Followed.

Remembered.

I do not think there is anything wrong with that.

Perhaps it would even be sad never to want it.

But there is an enormous difference between wanting to be seen and needing other people’s gaze to know who we are.

Almost fifty years after Sweet Revenge, the bargain is still on the table.

Only now the Devil does not have to knock at the door.

He is already in the phone.

And he keeps calling.

Follow me.

Freedom does not begin when we stop hearing that voice.

It begins when we no longer need to follow it blindly.
