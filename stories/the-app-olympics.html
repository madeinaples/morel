The App Olympics

I Stopped Auditioning.

Gay dating apps are supposed to help people meet.

In theory.

In practice, after more than ten years in London, I came to realise they resemble an Olympic competition. There is the age hurdle, the heavily edited photo throw, the obstacle course of faceless profiles, and the most prestigious event of all: turning up to a date looking nothing like the person in the pictures.

Some men still use photographs taken twelve years ago.

Not especially good photographs. Just photographs from another geological era, when they had more hair, fewer wrinkles, and probably an internet connection that made a strange noise before going online.

I can understand the fear of getting older.

What I understand less is the strategy.

Sooner or later, after all, you have to meet. You cannot arrive at someone’s home twelve years late and hope that soft lighting will take care of the rest.

One evening, when I was living in Fulham, I started talking to a man on an app.

His profile looked interesting. I do not remember the conversation being particularly dazzling, but on dating apps you do not need to be Oscar Wilde. Managing to write a complete sentence with a verb is often enough.

I asked him for more pictures.

He avoided the question.

I should have stopped there. But when you are lonely, curious, or simply bored, you sometimes give reality more credit than it deserves.

He invited me to his place.

The moment he opened the door, I knew the man standing in front of me was not the man in the photograph.

He was not an older version.

He had not gained weight.

He had not changed his hairstyle.

He was an entirely different person.

The most surprising thing, however, was not the deception. It was how relaxed he seemed.

He invited me inside as though everything were perfectly normal, as though I had no eyes, or as though I were so desperate I would have sex with a wardrobe, provided the wardrobe had replied to my message.

No explanation.

No apology.

No embarrassment.

It was as though the photograph had been nothing more than an administrative detail, a decorative image with no connection whatsoever to the product being delivered.

At first, I tried to remain polite.

It is a flaw I have always had. Even in dreadful situations, I try to negotiate, avoid humiliating anyone, and find a civilised way out. Being rude does not come naturally to me.

But he began moving closer and touching me while my entire body was pulling away.

At that point, I told him clearly to stop.

Then I told him to let me leave.

I was not polite.

I was sharp.

And it was necessary.

I walked out of that house with a feeling that was difficult to describe. It was not only anger. I felt hollowed out because I had been completely deceived and forced to become a version of myself I did not recognise.

I had needed to be harsh in order to defend a boundary that should have been obvious.

For a while, I stopped using the app.

Of course, I knew not everyone was like him. There are honest people, genuine men, people who show their real face and accept the possibility that they may not be someone else’s type.

But experiences like that leave you with a question.

Perhaps some people believe that men who are less attractive, older, or lonelier are easy targets.

Perhaps they assume we are desperate enough to accept whoever turns up at the door.

Perhaps they imagine loneliness erases taste, desire, and dignity.

It does not.

Being alone does not mean being available for anything.

Not being perfect does not mean having to feel grateful to anyone who chooses us.

And desire is not born from gratitude.

Eventually, I returned to the apps. Because, despite everything, real people do exist. And because it would be unfair to leave the field entirely to those using other people’s photographs, imaginary biographies, and measurements treated like academic qualifications.

I did not stop looking.

I stopped auditioning.
