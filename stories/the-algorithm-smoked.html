The Algorithm Smoked

Before Spotify and TikTok, success was already being measured. The data simply had a body: it danced, bought records, changed stations.

We like to tell ourselves the story this way.

Once, music was free.
Then the algorithm arrived.

Back then, apparently, all you had to do was record a good song. Someone heard it, fell in love with it and perhaps you became famous.

Now there is Spotify, TikTok, playlists, numbers, skips, seconds of attention.

Nice story.

Probably false.

The algorithm existed in the Seventies and Eighties too.

It just smoked, had a drink at the bar and, at two in the morning, stood behind the decks in a nightclub.

It watched the dancefloor.

Put on a record.

People kept dancing.

Good.

Put on another one.

The floor emptied.

Bad.

No dashboard required.

There were fifty people in front of you telling you exactly the same thing.

It is one of the most interesting things about disco and, even more, about Italo disco.

Much of the music we now treat as a small piece of European cultural heritage was born inside a system far less romantic than we like to remember.

You made it.
You tested it.
You watched.

If it worked, you pushed harder.

Clubs could become laboratories. DJs were antennas. Record shops picked up signals. Importers sensed that something was happening before anyone was entirely sure what to call it.

Then came radio.

Maybe television.

And somewhere along the way, someone had to decide what face all of this should have.

Because today, when we say Italo disco, an image appears almost instantly.

Synthesisers. Neon. Palm trees. Impossible jackets. Artificial sunsets. Sports cars. English names pronounced by Italians.

An America imagined from Milan.

A California that may actually have been Rimini.

But many of the people making those records did not wake up one morning and think:

“Today we invent Italo disco.”

They made records.

Then someone collected them, distributed them, carried them outside Italy. And that rather untidy mass of productions began to acquire an identity.

That is where it gets interesting.

Because a musical genre is not born only when someone creates a sound.

It is also born when someone gives it a name.

When someone puts together things that had previously been separate.

When a distributor decides those Italian records might work in Germany.

When a DJ plays them.

When a sleeve suggests that the boy photographed in front of a Lamborghini belongs to a world much larger than the studio where he probably recorded the track.

Pop has always needed mythology.

It is just that mythology used to take up physical space.

Twelve inches, for example.

A record sleeve could tell you a story before the record ever touched the turntable.

You looked at a name.

A photograph.

A typeface.

And you had already started imagining who that person might be.

Sometimes you knew almost nothing about the artist.

That was precisely the beauty of it.

There were no thirty interviews on YouTube.

You did not know what they had eaten that morning.

You had not seen their kitchen.

You did not know the dog.

You had a photograph.

A name.

Three minutes and forty-two seconds.

You built the rest yourself.

Perhaps that is why some artists seemed enormous even when they were not.

Scarcity produced imagination.

And sometimes it produced legend.

There are records that became cult objects despite barely circulating at the time. Small pressings, local distribution, promotional copies, tiny labels.

Today that rarity can look almost like proof of greatness.

Perhaps the record was simply badly distributed.

And that is wonderful.

Because it means part of pop history was built by mistakes as well.

A record that never reached the right shops.

A song that worked in one club and failed in another.

A sleeve that became iconic by accident.

An Italian producer inventing an American name.

A German importer spotting a market.

Then MTV arrived.

And having a sound was no longer enough.

You had to be visible.

The song needed a body, clothes, colours, gestures.

The music video no longer simply accompanied the record.

It built the character.

Pop had understood something that social media would later push much further.

We do not sell songs alone.

We sell people.

Or, more precisely, the idea of a person.

So perhaps the difference between then and now is not that there was no algorithm.

It is that we could not see it.

It was scattered.

A piece in the producer’s head.

One in the DJ’s hands.

One in the distributor’s phone call.

One in the radio programmer.

One in the photograph chosen for the sleeve.

One in the legs of the people deciding whether to keep dancing.

Today all of this has become a number.

We can know how many seconds you listened before giving up on a song.

Which city you were in.

What time it was.

Which song you heard before it.

It is more precise.

I am not sure it is more interesting.

Because that old machine contained one variable that was very difficult to measure.

Error.

Someone could believe in a record that did not appear to work.

A DJ could play it again the following week.

A record-shop owner could recommend it.

A local radio station could turn it into something.

An almost unknown Italian record could cross a border and suddenly find its audience in Germany.

And forty years later, someone could recover it and decide it was worth hearing again.

Perhaps this is what we have lost more than the absence of the algorithm itself.

The time required for something to become important after first appearing irrelevant.

Today we constantly ask music to prove, immediately, that it works.

The dancefloor wanted an answer too.

But at least you had to wait until the record finished.
