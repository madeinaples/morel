Behind the Wall

Sometimes we do not have to demolish a defence before we can come closer.

Author’s note: this story explores intimacy, vulnerability and consent between adults. Some details have been changed to protect the privacy of the people involved.

It was a Sunday afternoon, one of those suspended hours when the city slows down without ever quite stopping.

I had not opened that app for a while. I was scrolling without much attention when I stopped on a profile.

He said he was straight. Looking for women. Trans women. Not men.

I kept scrolling.

There was not much to interpret.

I was not what he was looking for.

Ten minutes later, a message arrived.

It was him.

He wrote politely. Almost cautiously. He did not seem frightened by desire so much as by everything surrounding it: being seen, looking, having to explain, perhaps having to explain even to himself.

He wanted distance. Protection. Something between us.

I did not ask why.

Some questions, when asked too early, do not help us know someone. They simply force them to defend themselves better.

When he arrived, we barely greeted one another. We had already spoken enough to know the boundaries.

No one had to persuade anyone. No one had to prove anything.

The door closed.

Between us there was a wall.

It did not matter much what it was made of. Wood. Fabric. Darkness. Fear.

Sometimes the strongest walls do not require bricks.

He needed it.

And I decided to leave it there.

We often treat defences as enemies of intimacy. Tear down your walls. Let go. Show yourself completely.

It sounds excellent, usually in white lettering over a photograph of the sea.

Reality is less elegant.

Some people can come closer precisely because they know they are allowed to stop.

Because there is a door. A distance. A part of themselves they do not have to hand over immediately.

In the dark I began noticing small things: a change in breathing, a movement of the hand, a pause that seemed almost accidental.

I did not know what any of it meant.

And I did not try to decide for him.

Breathing is a strange thing. In daylight it belongs to the body’s background noise. In darkness it can feel like a confession.

You can control a sentence. Prepare an answer. Decide where to look.

Breathing is less disciplined.

Sometimes it tells something before we are ready to say it.

His hand moved again. More slowly this time.

I could not tell whether he was reaching for something or simply checking that I was still there.

I stayed still.

Sometimes the answer is not to do something. It is to give the other person time to decide.

The wall held back images. It did not hold back everything else: breathing, hesitation, tiny changes in distance.

He had built a barrier in order to feel protected.

But a barrier is not necessarily a rejection.

It can be a condition.

A way of saying: I can be here, but like this.

At some point, what had first felt like distance began to resemble attention.

The wall was still there. It had not become lower. It had not collapsed.

And yet it stopped feeling like the thing that divided us.

Sometimes trust does not arrive with a declaration. It arrives in tiny quantities: a pause, a breath held for a second, the way somebody moves closer and then stops, the fact that nobody pushes.

Later, he messaged me. He thanked me for the discretion, for respecting what he had asked for, and for something he described as far more intense than he had expected.

The paradox stayed with me.

He had arrived asking for a barrier.

And that barrier had allowed something through.

A week later he wrote again. This time to talk. Not about the encounter. About work, life, writing, thoughts.

The wall was gone.

Or perhaps it had simply changed form.

For years I thought intimacy began when two people managed to tear down their defences.

I am not so sure anymore.

Some defences are useful, at least at first. They allow us to approach without feeling completely exposed.

The problem is not having a wall.

The problem begins when the wall becomes a prison we no longer know how to open even from the inside.

As long as a defence allows us to remain present, it can have a function. As long as we can choose when to come closer and when to stop, it is not necessarily distance.

It is a boundary.

I still do not know exactly what he was looking for that afternoon. Perhaps an experience. An answer. A confirmation. Perhaps simply a space where he did not have to know immediately who he was supposed to be.

It does not matter.

Some questions work better when they remain open.

We never tore the wall down. We did not need to.

It simply became permeable enough to let something true pass through.

Perhaps this too is a right we rarely talk about.

The right to come closer without handing over everything at once.

We do not have to be completely open in order to be sincere. We do not have to tell everything to deserve trust. And we do not have to force someone’s defences in order to prove that they can trust us.

Sometimes trust begins precisely because nobody tries to tear them down.

Later another bus passed outside. The window trembled exactly as before.

The room was the same.

I was not.
