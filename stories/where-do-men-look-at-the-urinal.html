Where Do Men Look at the Urinal?

A short treatise on masculinity in front of a tiled wall.

The idea came to me yesterday, in an airport toilet.

I was standing at a urinal with my phone in my hand. Not because I had anything urgent to read, but because, at that particular moment, looking at a screen seemed far less complicated than deciding where to put my eyes.

The most embarrassing detail is that I wasn’t observing the other men.

I was observing myself pretending not to observe anyone.

I had adopted that serious expression we use when we want people to believe we are dealing with something important. I might have been checking the international markets, coordinating an evacuation or communicating with the Prime Minister.

In reality, I was probably staring at my home screen.

At that moment, the phone was not a phone.

It was an official certificate of disinterest.

Its purpose was to inform the men around me that I had matters far more urgent to deal with than their existence forty centimetres away from mine.

The men’s toilet is one of the few places where men capable of running companies, driving double-decker buses and discussing NATO suddenly lose every social skill they possess.

All it takes is for a stranger to occupy the next urinal and we become deer caught in headlights.

The eyes immediately begin searching for neutral territory.

The wall.

The tiles.

The brand name on the flush.

The instructions for the automatic sensor.

Anything, as long as they do not drift seven centimetres too far to the left.

Some men study the grout between the tiles with a level of concentration they have never devoted to their tax return.

Others stare at the ceiling, perhaps searching for spiritual guidance.

Others check their phones with the grave expression of a Foreign Secretary during an international crisis.

Nobody, of course, is actually reading anything.

We are all playing the part of men with extremely urgent matters to resolve on WhatsApp, waiting for the person beside us to finish and return the area to international neutrality.

There is also an unwritten rule, probably older than Magna Carta.

If there are six empty urinals and only one man in the room, you do not choose the one next to him.

Ever.

You leave at least one empty space between you.

That unused urinal is not wasted space.

It is ceramic Switzerland.

A buffer zone.

A humanitarian corridor.

A peace agreement between two men who do not know each other and would very much like to keep it that way.

If you enter an almost empty toilet and choose the position directly beside the only person there, you have not simply made a poor spatial decision.

You have declared war.

The other man will not think, “Perhaps he didn’t notice.”

He will think, “Who is this individual? What does he want? Why here? Why now?”

Western masculinity can survive many things.

It cannot survive the incorrect choice of urinal.

Naturally, there are several distinct species.

There is the Cartographer, who studies the arrangement of the tiles as though he has been commissioned to design a new Underground line.

There is the Foreign Secretary, permanently occupied with his phone.

There is the Sprinter, who enters, completes the operation and disappears before anyone can officially register his presence.

There is the Illegal Occupier, the man who chooses the adjacent position even when the entire room is empty.

And then there is the Conversationalist.

The real public danger.

The Conversationalist enters, takes up position and says something.

“Long day?”

“Terrible weather.”

“Flight delayed?”

It is difficult to explain how threatening an innocent question can sound when it comes from a man with his trousers open.

At that moment, you do not want to discuss the weather.

You do not want to talk about your flight.

You do not want to know where he has come from or where he is going.

You simply want to complete the operation while preserving your institutional dignity.

The men’s toilet is also one of the few places where silence is not regarded as rudeness.

It is an essential service.

A gesture of respect.

A form of mutual assistance.

We can leave, wash our hands and exchange a small nod. A minimal, almost administrative movement meaning:

“I saw you.”
“So did I.”
“We will never speak of what has just happened.”

Then each of us returns to his life.

And yet, behind all that pretending not to look, something more complicated is taking place.

Men compare themselves constantly.

Body, age, confidence, posture, height, hair, clothes, success, desirability.

They do it in bars, gyms, offices and on apps.

And naturally, they do it in toilets too, precisely in the place where they pretend most convincingly that comparison does not exist.

Perhaps we are not afraid of being looked at.

Perhaps we are afraid that someone’s gaze will confirm something we were already thinking about ourselves.

That we are too old.

Too fat.

Too thin.

Too insecure.

Not enough of something.

We spend half our lives wanting to be seen and the other half desperately hoping nobody looks in our direction.

Yesterday, I had my phone in my hand.

The other men were staring at the wall.

Nobody was looking at anyone.

Or at least, that was the official version.

For thirty seconds, we were all there in a row, silent, concentrating on the tiles and working hard to defend our extremely fragile boundaries.

Group therapy without a therapist.

Then we zipped up, washed our hands and returned to the world.

Where we immediately started measuring ourselves against one another again.
