Have You Eaten?

Sometimes affection insists. Sometimes it gives space. The difficult part is recognising it when it does not speak our language.

Entering a Neapolitan home means facing a series of questions nobody really expects you to answer.

The first arrives before you have taken your coat off:

“Can I make you a coffee?”

The hour is irrelevant. Morning? Coffee. Afternoon? Coffee. Eleven at night after seven courses? Obviously coffee.

In Naples, coffee is not a drink. It is an entry procedure.

Then comes the important question:

“Have you eaten?”

This also appears to be a question. It is not.

If you say no, the kitchen enters a state of national mobilisation. If you say yes, somebody looks suspicious.

“When?”

“An hour ago.”

“And what did you eat?”

Your previous meal is now under review.

Eventually someone says the phrase that has ended many sensible evenings:

“Fine. Just a little pasta.”

In Naples, just a little pasta is not a quantity. It is the beginning of operations.

The problem of emotional languages

For years I thought this insistence was simply our way of being: affectionate, noisy and not especially interested in the possibility that “no, thank you” might be final.

Then I moved to England.

You entered a house. Your coat was taken discreetly. You sat down. Everything appeared calm and under control.

Then came the question:

“Would you like a cup of tea?”

I knew the Italian ritual of the polite first refusal, so I said:

“I’m fine, thank you.”

They smiled. “All right.”

End of negotiation.

I waited.

Nobody insisted. No aunt emerged from a kitchen holding a cake. Five minutes later the host was drinking tea and I was studying the plate of biscuits like a naturalist observing an endangered species.

In England, even a biscuit can become a diplomatic matter.

At first I translated that distance into my own emotional language.

And in my language, if you do not insist, perhaps you do not care enough.

It was a bad translation.

The English people around me simply tended to assume that if I said I did not want something, I probably did not want it.

What I read as coldness could be respect.

What they might read as intrusion could, to me, be care.

Many emotional misunderstandings begin here: we mistake for absence what simply does not resemble our own way of loving.

When food means something else

In Naples, food carries a memory longer than any single family.

The city knew war, bombing, hunger, poverty and black markets. There was not always much to offer.

But what existed could be shared.

“Where four can eat, eight can eat too.”

It did not mean food multiplied. It meant it was divided.

Perhaps that is why abundance did not erase the gesture.

Offering still means: I have seen you. You have entered my space. You are, in some small way, my responsibility now.

It is beautiful.

And it can be suffocating.

Both things can be true.

Because affection has to learn one unromantic but essential lesson:

care does not stop being care when it respects a boundary.

A place at the table

I understood this properly one Christmas in England.

I knew very few people and thought I might spend the day alone.

Some people I had only recently met simply said:

“You’re not spending Christmas alone. Come with us.”

No grand speech. No dramatic interrogation. They simply made it clear that there was already a place for me at the table.

That was when I stopped asking who was more hospitable.

It was the wrong question.

In Naples somebody filled your plate.

In England somebody had made space before you arrived.

Different gestures.

The same sentence, spoken with different accents:

“You can stay here.”

Not all love insists

Growing up means learning — or at least trying to learn — to recognise affection even when it does not take the form we know best.

Some people call three times to check you got home. Others assume that if you need them, you will call.

Some cook. Some listen. Some bring something without asking. Some ask before entering.

None of these forms is automatically superior.

The problem begins when we turn our emotional dialect into the only dictionary allowed.

Not all love insists. And not everything that insists is love.

Perhaps the ideal hospitality is still an English house with a Neapolitan kitchen: nobody invades your space, but somebody has put the water on anyway.

The moka murmurs. The kettle clicks.

Different sounds.

When they matter, the same meaning:

“Stay a little longer.”
