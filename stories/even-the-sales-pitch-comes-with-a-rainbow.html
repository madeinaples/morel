Even the Sales Pitch Comes with a Rainbow

When the need to feel accepted becomes a sales opportunity.

“When did you last go dancing? When did you last go on holiday?”

Those questions could start a conversation. I might tell you I’m short of money, that my friends have moved away, that going out alone sometimes feels like hard work. Or that I’m happy at home and don’t owe anyone an explanation.

But if you already have the answer — you don’t go out because you’re uncomfortable with your body — then the conversation is over before I’ve had a chance to speak.

You asked me a question only to steer me towards what you sell.

That is what makes me angry. How easily an entire life gets reduced to a physical problem, preferably one that can be solved by buying a service. And how confidently that problem gets assigned to gay men, as though sharing a sexual orientation meant sharing a diagnosis.

A body that isn’t gym-trained is not a neglected body.

I could stop right there. Because buried in that confusion is a judgement that slips past almost unnoticed. If you haven’t worked hard enough on your appearance, you’ve let yourself go. If you don’t show yourself, perhaps you know you’re not presentable. And if you don’t take part in the life someone else has chosen as the measure of happiness, something must be wrong with you.

Can we really not imagine another explanation?

Being gay doesn’t mean wanting to go clubbing. It doesn’t mean having money to travel, being outgoing or constantly looking for someone. It doesn’t even mean feeling bad about your body. Some people like themselves without being sculpted. Others spend months in the gym and still don’t.

A question should help you discover who is in front of you. If it only confirms the problem you know how to sell a solution to, you’re listening to your market.

The term is pink money. Our purchases, our spending power, the market that has grown around us.

Spending money doesn’t scandalise me. I want to book a holiday, pay a trainer, choose a night out. I want people to be paid for their work, too. I don’t expect every business serving LGBT people to operate as a charity.

What interests me is how you get to my wallet.

You can offer me something I want. You can help me work towards a goal I’ve chosen. But you can also plant a doubt: perhaps you don’t feel included because you haven’t improved yourself enough yet.

At that point, the product changes. The price starts to include a promise no professional can guarantee: afterwards, you’ll be desired, you’ll feel comfortable, people will see you.

That may be precisely what the customer is looking for. All the more reason to be clear about what you are actually offering.

A training programme can help me train. It cannot make someone love me. It cannot make a judgemental environment welcoming. It cannot promise I’ll stop feeling out of place.

The uncomfortable part is that the person selling this promise may be another gay man.

We like to think the problem always comes from outside. The company using the rainbow. The brand discovering inclusion when it becomes profitable. It’s harder to recognise the same mechanism in someone who speaks like us, spends time in our spaces and knows some of our fears.

We expect a different kind of understanding from him. That is why his words can get closer.

“I know how you feel” can be a comforting sentence. It can also lower the defences of someone who needs to be understood. It depends on what happens next.

Belonging to the same community doesn’t automatically make us considerate, competent or free of self-interest. We can support one another. We can also hurt one another. And we can reproduce, among ourselves, the very conditions of acceptance we thought we had escaped.

Outside, we were told to be less visible. Inside, sometimes it seems that being seen requires us to become younger, more toned, more marketable.

At that point, a welcome starts to look like an audition.

Not everyone encounters these messages from the same comfortable position.

Some people watch a video, roll their eyes and scroll on. Others watch it after being rejected, after a night when nobody spoke to them, after deleting a photograph because they couldn’t bear to look at themselves.

You don’t know what kind of day they’re having when you reach them.

For some, their relationship with their body is uncomplicated. For others, it is tangled up with years of shame and silence, with trying to feel entitled, at last, to exist without apologising. These are serious things. They don’t become less serious because we discuss them in a short video with cheerful music underneath.

The risk is that the message lands like this: if I’m still alone, I haven’t tried hard enough. If nobody chooses me, I haven’t taken care of myself. If I feel bad, I still need to work on myself.

And everyone else? The way we treat one another? The spaces we call inclusive? When do they enter the conversation?

If the answer is always to change the individual, nobody has to question the welcome they receive.

I won’t pretend appearance plays no part in desire. It does. We have tastes, preferences and attractions that cannot be legislated into existence.

But there is an enormous distance between not desiring someone and treating them as a person of lesser worth. We can choose who we sleep with without turning everyone else into a body that needs fixing.

And we can love the gym without making it the place where you earn the right to go to the beach.

Someone helping me should also be able to say this: you can start living now. While you train, while you change, while you decide you don’t want to change. You don’t have to put everything on hold for a future version of yourself.

The one who, conveniently, is never quite ready.

I left a critical comment under one of these videos. Later, I couldn’t see it anymore. I don’t know why it disappeared.

But the question remains, with or without that comment, with or without the name of the person who posted the video. It concerns how we speak to people when we know they might be vulnerable. It concerns what we are prepared to suggest in order to get their attention.

I’d like to ask for help without being told, once again, that I’m defective. I’d like the word “care” to leave room for rest, health, limits and age. For a body that doesn’t have to be displayed to prove it has been loved.

My body is not an application for membership.

Before you offer me a programme, at least let me answer your question.

Maybe I haven’t been dancing.

Maybe I just wanted someone to listen.

— MOREL
