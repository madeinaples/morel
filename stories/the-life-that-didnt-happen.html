The Life That Didn't Happen

Sometimes a missed possibility doesn't tell us where we should have lived. It tells us we already knew we could become someone else.

I always knew my city was too small for me.

Not because there was anything wrong with it.

It was simply too small for all the versions of myself I hadn't met yet.

When I was seventeen, an aunt who lived in America came to visit.

She looked at me, spoke to my mother and said something very simple:

Let him come to America. He'll be happy with us.

It didn't happen.

I was still a minor, and some decisions belonged to adults.

For years I could have told this as the story of the missed opportunity.

The American life I never lived. The door that never opened. The great fork in the road.

But I don't think that's the point anymore.

Someone had seen something

That aunt couldn't have known who I would become.

I probably didn't know either.

But she had sensed something: perhaps I needed space.

Not necessarily America.

Space.

To try. To change. To become a version of myself I couldn't yet fully imagine in the place where I was born.

It's strange when someone sees a possibility in us before we have the words to describe it.

They don't hand us a future.

They simply show us that another future exists.

The alternative life

Sometimes I think about the boy who would have left.

What kind of English would he have spoken?

Who would he have met?

Who would he have loved?

Would he have stayed?

Would he have come back?

Would he have become braver, or simply different?

I'll never know.

And that's exactly the point.

The lives we didn't live are perfect because they never had time to disappoint us.

We can furnish them however we like.

But they aren't necessarily the lives we were supposed to live.

A place won't save you

For years I associated change with leaving.

Elsewhere seemed to mean freedom.

And sometimes it does: changing cities, countries, languages or people can give us the space we were missing.

But a place doesn't grant us permission to become someone.

It can make it easier.

It can speed things up.

It can even force us to change.

But sooner or later, we have to give ourselves permission.

I didn't go to America at seventeen.

But the idea that I might belong somewhere else had already entered the room.

It never really left.

We can change more than once

There was something else I didn't know then.

I thought becoming yourself was a destination.

You start here. You go through something. Eventually you arrive at yourself.

Convenient.

Shame it doesn't work like that.

We can become another version of ourselves whenever we decide to.

Not only because we move somewhere else.

We can change by staying.

We can change by returning.

We can change at sixty after spending decades telling ourselves the role had already been cast.

And yes, we can change for the worse too. Evolution comes with no warranty.

But we change.

That life didn't happen

The American life of my seventeen-year-old imagination didn't happen.

And that's all right.

Perhaps its job wasn't to happen.

Perhaps its job was to show me that elsewhere existed.

That the geography I was born into wasn't the boundary of the person I could become.

An aunt had sensed it.

It took me a little longer.

Then, at some point, I stopped waiting for someone else to say:

Let him go.

I decided for myself.

And perhaps adulthood really begins there: when we stop asking life for permission to become our next version.
