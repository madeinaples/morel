An evolving narrative

The right to…

Duties support us. Rights let us breathe.

We were taught many duties.

Be responsible. Be present. Keep your word. Take care of the people you love. Answer for your choices.

And rightly so.

Duties support us.

But a life made only of duties becomes rigid.

Rights are the space that still allows us to breathe, change direction and remain alive.

The problem does not begin when we have duties.

It begins when we forget that we also have rights.

The right to change our mind. To stop wanting what we once wanted. To pause. Not to be strong all the time. To disappoint somebody without betraying them. To carry the traces of what we have lived without being condemned to remain the person we were then.

This is not an invitation to do whatever we please.

It is almost the opposite.

It is an attempt to understand which responsibilities genuinely belong to us, and which obligations we keep obeying only because somebody once told us who we were supposed to become.

The duty not to disappoint

Some duties are never handed to us in writing. They do not appear in contracts and they never expire, yet they can accompany us for a lifetime.

One of them is the duty not to disappoint.

When we are young it can take ordinary forms. A brother who seems to be doing better. A cousin held up as the example. A friend’s child who has taken the right road while you still seem to be searching for yours.

Repeated often enough, comparison stops being comparison.

It becomes a measure.

And if that measure teaches you that you are worth little — or nothing — something more serious can happen than the words themselves: you begin to believe them.

I believed them for years.

When you think you must prove your worth, many choices change meaning. You are no longer simply trying to succeed. You are looking for evidence.

A result that says those words were wrong. A person who chooses you and proves you are desirable. A job, success, body, home or relationship that can testify in your favour.

It becomes a trial we keep conducting inside ourselves long after the prosecution has left the room.

Proof that was never for them

For me, one of the first cracks in that belief came when I opened a wine bar.

I had no previous experience giving me permission to think: I know how to do this.

I had to learn. Study. Work. Make mistakes. Begin again. Build it from zero.

For a long time I thought of that experience as redemption. In part it was. But now I see it differently.

The important thing was not proving to other people that I could succeed.

It was discovering that I could trust myself.

Then came something far less spectacular: living alone. With little money. Without asking. Trying to build a dignified life anyway.

There was no applause. Nobody handed out prizes for paying your bills, making it through a difficult day or waking up and continuing.

And yet perhaps that is where I understood something.

Success is not always reaching higher.
Sometimes it is simply discovering: I can stand on my own feet.

The right to stop

For years I tried to prove that I was not worth zero.

Now I can see the paradox: by constantly searching for evidence to the contrary, I was still granting that old sentence the right to judge me.

Eventually the question changed.

Not: how do I prove how much I am worth?

But: why should I still have to prove it?

I do not have to prove anything to anyone anymore.
And that, more than any achievement, has been the real victory.

It does not mean becoming immune to judgement, or believing we are always right.

It means no longer building a life as a response to other people’s expectations.

Perhaps this is one of the rights we forget most easily:

the right to stop competing.

Because we can spend years chasing results we think are desires when they are really replies.

Replies to a parent, a teacher, an ex, a friend, somebody who made us feel insufficient a very long time ago.

So it is worth asking:

How much of what we want truly belongs to us, and how much is still trying to prove something to somebody?

Perhaps some victories begin exactly when we stop needing to win.

This story is not finished

The right to… will grow.

The right to change. To give people their proper weight. Not to disown who we were. To inhabit our body. To change a dream. To become without already having a life CV to present.

Some pages will be added. Others rewritten. A few may disappear.

Because writing about the right to change inside a project that insists on remaining identical would be a rather convenient contradiction.

We have the right to change.
These pages do too.
