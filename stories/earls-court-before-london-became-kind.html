Earl’s Court, Before London Became Kind

Changing a neighbourhood is inevitable. Erasing the people who gave it its identity should not be.

There are neighbourhoods we have lived in, and neighbourhoods that, without asking permission, keep living inside us.

For me, Earl’s Court belongs to the second kind.

It was my first London home.

Victorian façades. Hotels carved out of old houses. Basement flats. Late-night supermarkets. The station gathering residents, migrants, students, tourists, solitary men, couples and people who seemed to have arrived yesterday or to have been leaving for years.

Earl’s Court had one quality I understood before I understood its history:

it did not immediately ask who you were.

I do not know whether that was tolerance or simply administrative overload of the soul.

But when you arrive from another country, even not being questioned can feel like welcome.

Freedom before it looked normal

At the time I knew very little about what Earl’s Court had been.

I only knew that there I could exist with less need to translate myself.

Later I understood that this ease had not been born easy.

For decades the area had absorbed temporary lives: migrants, Australians and New Zealanders, students, workers, people in bedsits and divided houses.

The façades could look respectable. Inside, life was much less orderly.

And it is often in the least orderly places that somebody finally manages to breathe.

Before Soho became the most visible centre of gay London, Earl’s Court was one of the city’s important meeting places for gay men. Pubs such as The Boltons and the Coleherne were part of that geography.

This history began before 1967, when sex between men was partially decriminalised in England and Wales.

Partially.

A small word capable of containing a great deal of fear.

A pub could be more than somewhere to drink. It could be a network, an address, a confirmation that you were not alone.

And darkened windows were not simply atmosphere. They were protection.

When refuge is not enough

The history of those places is not romantic.

It should not become romantic simply because time has given the photographs a beautiful grain.

In 1993 Colin Ireland murdered five gay men. The Coleherne was among the places he used to look for victims.

The vulnerability of those men did not come from their desire. It came from the social price they could pay if that desire became visible.

The law had changed. Respect from institutions took longer.

London was not yet the city that now likes to photograph itself beneath a rainbow, preferably with a bank logo discreetly visible in the corner.

Freedom existed. But often it had a precise address.

The neighbourhood changes. The trace remains.

Earl’s Court was also one of London’s great stages. The Exhibition Centre, opened in 1937, hosted concerts, fairs, sport and spectacles on a scale that made the surrounding streets feel temporarily borrowed by the rest of the city.

Then it closed. Then it was demolished.

Cities specialise in this kind of unsentimental magic.

They demolish buildings, change signs, raise rents, clean façades and then pretend the neighbourhood had always looked that way.

The gay geography changed too. Venues closed. Meeting places moved. Apps shifted encounters from streets and bars to screens.

That is progress.

It is also loss.

The two things can exist in the same sentence without cancelling one another.

Who gets to decide what remains?

A city has to change. Pretending otherwise would be absurd.

We need homes, investment, services, infrastructure and new possibilities.

But building the new should not automatically require the removal of the people who gave the old its identity.

The problem is not transformation. It is who can afford to remain once the transformation has happened.

A neighbourhood is not merely the sum of its buildings.

It is the people who lived in them. The businesses where they met. The communities that found space there when other places gave them none.

If we preserve the façades and lose all of that, we have not restored a neighbourhood.

We have preserved its costume.

Gentrification is often described as if it were weather: something that simply arrives.

But choices are made. Money moves. Investment decides what is valuable. Planning decides what can be built. Rent decides who can stay.

A community can disappear without anybody formally ordering it to leave.

Sometimes all it takes is making its own home unaffordable.

Perhaps people can be gentrified too

This is where Earl’s Court stops being only a story about a neighbourhood for me.

Because something similar can happen to people.

We grow. We adapt. We become more polished, safer, more successful, more presentable.

Necessity renovates us. Money can. Love can. Fear can. So can the need to be accepted.

But who decides which parts of us have to disappear so that the new version looks better?

Family. Society. Work. A partner. An audience. Sometimes ourselves.

We remove what looks inconvenient. We modernise what seems embarrassing. We keep the façade that receives approval.

Until, perhaps, we become perfectly coherent.

Perfectly acceptable.

And no longer entirely at home inside ourselves.

Perhaps we have been gentrified.

Changing is not the problem.

MOREL itself begins from the idea that we are not obliged to remain the same.

But change should not require us to expel every trace of the person who brought us here.

The right to forget fear

There is a paradox in successful freedoms: eventually they begin to look natural.

And that is partly the point.

A younger person should be able to hold someone’s hand, enter a venue, name their love — or decide not to name it — without carrying a permanent history lesson on their shoulders.

Freedom should not have to be lived as a permanent commemoration.

We do not have to ask the dead for permission to be happy.

But we can remember.

We have the right to live a freedom without feeling indebted. And we have the responsibility not to pretend it appeared by itself.

When I arrived in Earl’s Court, I was already benefiting from the possibility of explaining myself less.

I did not know the people who had made that easier.

But I was living inside the consequences of their lives.

Earl’s Court has changed.

So have I.

Cities have the right to change. People do too.

But changing does not mean having to erase every trace.

Traces do not oblige us to remain who we were. They stop us pretending we were born as the version we show today.
