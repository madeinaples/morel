Earl’s Court, Before London Became Kind

The neighbourhood that offered freedom when freedom still came at a price.

There are neighbourhoods in which we have lived, and neighbourhoods that, without asking permission, have continued living inside us.

For me, Earl’s Court belongs to the second kind.

It was my first London home.

Before I properly understood London, I learnt those streets: the pale façades of Victorian houses, hotels carved out of former private residences, steps descending towards basement flats and small supermarkets open late, essential when you have not yet discovered that in Britain dinner can begin at the exact hour when Italians are still deciding where to have an aperitivo.

Everyone passed through the area around the station: elegant residents, students, immigrants, tourists dragging suitcases large enough to contain a second family, solitary men, discreet couples, people who had just arrived and people who appeared to have been preparing to leave for several years.

Earl’s Court had a particular quality. It did not immediately ask who you were. Not necessarily because it cared deeply about your freedom. Perhaps it was simply too busy managing everybody else’s.

When you have arrived from another country, even not being questioned can feel like a form of welcome.

At the time, I knew nothing of the neighbourhood’s full history. I only knew that those streets allowed me to exist without continually explaining myself. It was only later that I understood that this apparent ease had been won.

Before the houses

Before nineteenth-century expansion, the area consisted largely of farmland and open ground at the edge of the city.

During the nineteenth century, urban development reached this part of west London. Squares, residential terraces, stucco houses and private gardens appeared. Earl’s Court Square and the surrounding streets were created for an orderly, respectable society, preferably one protected from surprises.

Naturally, London did what London always does. It changed everything.

Large houses were divided. Rooms became flats, boarding houses and bedsits. Behind elegant façades, temporary lives multiplied: shared kitchens, bathrooms on landings and people learning to call home a room just large enough for a bed, a chair and several badly folded hopes.

That transience became one of the neighbourhood’s defining characteristics. Earl’s Court did not belong only to those born there or wealthy enough to occupy an entire house. It also belonged to people who arrived, stayed for a few months or years and then moved on.

After the Second World War, new European migrants arrived alongside thousands of young Australians and New Zealanders. Part of the area became known as Kangaroo Valley.

They lived in furnished rooms, accepted temporary jobs and looked for compatriots in pubs, as migrants often do: they cross half the world and then experience an almost touching relief on meeting somebody capable of pronouncing the name of their hometown correctly.

The mixture did not necessarily make Earl’s Court harmonious. It made it permeable. In a city still governed by class, origin and respectability, the possibility of disappearing among other people could become a form of protection.

A capital within the capital

Before Soho became the most recognisable face of gay London, Earl’s Court was one of the city’s principal meeting places for gay men.

Pubs such as The Boltons, on Earl’s Court Road, and the Coleherne, in the Old Brompton Road area, were known as places frequented by gay men from the 1950s onwards.

This began before 1967, when sex between men was partially decriminalised in England and Wales.

Partially.

It is a small word containing a remarkable amount of fear. The law applied to consensual acts in private between two men over the age of twenty-one. It did not suddenly remove police surveillance, blackmail, dismissal from work, family rejection or public exposure.

In that context, a pub was not merely somewhere to drink. It was a place to meet people like oneself, exchange information, work out who could be trusted and feel part of something. Sometimes it was the only space in which a person could lower their guard, although rarely enough to abandon it completely.

The Coleherne became particularly famous during the 1970s. Its blacked-out windows, leather culture, international clientele and occasional famous visitors helped turn it into a symbol.

Today all of this risks looking very elegant: men in leather, music, smoke, black-and-white photographs, a few celebrities. The past, when observed for long enough, always begins dressing better than it did in real life.

Those windows, however, were not blacked out simply to create atmosphere. They were blacked out because being recognised could carry a serious cost.

Refuge and danger

Places offering protection can also attract those willing to exploit the vulnerability of others.

In 1993, Colin Ireland murdered five gay men after entering London’s leather scene. The Coleherne was one of the places he used to look for victims.

This part of the history should not be used to make the story darker. Reality was already dramatic enough without assistance from the writer.

Those men were more vulnerable because some concealed their private lives, hesitated to approach the police or feared that an investigation would publicly expose them. Their vulnerability was not created by being gay. It was created by the conditions in which society forced them to live.

For many years, relations with the police were difficult too. Raids, surveillance, harassment and public protests formed part of the community’s experience. The law may have stopped criminalising part of their private lives, but that did not mean institutions had suddenly learnt respect.

London was not yet the city it now enjoys presenting in advertising campaigns: open, cosmopolitan, reassuring, covered in rainbows every June, preferably with the logo of a bank printed discreetly in the corner.

It was a city in which freedom existed, but often within carefully defined boundaries: a doorway, a bar, a familiar street, a glance capable of telling you who might be a friend and who could represent danger.

The great spectacle

Earl’s Court was not only a centre of migrant and gay life. It was also one of London’s great popular stages.

The Earl’s Court Exhibition Centre opened in 1937 and became one of the city’s most recognisable venues. It hosted trade fairs, motor shows, sporting events, concerts and the London Boat Show, because London apparently considered it perfectly reasonable to transport enormous boats into the middle of a residential neighbourhood.

On event days, the streets changed rhythm. Visitors, salespeople, technicians, musicians and crowds poured towards the entrance. Then everything ended and the streets returned to their hotels, residents, pubs and people crossing the station with the expression of someone who had already missed a train and had no intention of giving the city the satisfaction of showing it.

The final show took place on 13 December 2014. The building was dismantled during 2015.

For almost a decade, an enormous section of the neighbourhood remained empty, suspended between schemes, opposition and promises of transformation. London often demolishes with tremendous confidence. It is when the city must decide what to build in place of what it has destroyed that it begins to look uncertain.

In December 2025, Kensington and Chelsea’s planning committee approved a new scheme involving housing, public spaces, cultural venues, offices and commercial uses, before referral to the Mayor of London.

Earl’s Court is preparing to change once again. That is what London continually does: it builds, demolishes and renames. Then it sells as innovation what it has just removed from memory.

When the scene moved

Between the late 1980s and the 1990s, the geography of gay London began to change.

The Boltons ceased operating as a gay pub in 1989. The Coleherne gradually lost the central role it had once occupied. New venues opened elsewhere and Soho became the more visible centre of the scene.

But neighbourhoods do not change overnight.

First one venue closes. Then another changes ownership. A rent increases. A boarding house becomes expensive flats. People continue meeting, but they must travel a little further.

Eventually somebody announces that the area is not what it used to be, as though everything vanished through absent-mindedness rather than through a precise sequence of economic decisions.

A venue can close without any law being passed against the community that used it. A community can be dispersed without anybody formally ordering it to leave. It is enough to make remaining there unaffordable.

Gentrification possesses that particular elegance. It does not raise its voice. It raises the rent.

My first London

By the time I arrived in Earl’s Court, many of these changes had already happened.

I did not know the neighbourhood of the 1970s. I did not see the Coleherne at the height of its fame. I did not walk those streets when entering the wrong pub, meeting the wrong person or being recognised by somebody could endanger an entire private life.

I encountered what remained: the divided houses, the hotels, the people passing through, the stairs descending towards basement flats, the illuminated windows at night and the sense that behind every door was a story that had begun in another country.

At the time, I did not think about the neighbourhood’s political memory. I was simply trying to build a life, understand London and find a direction without appearing completely lost, a skill the city demands from every newcomer and almost nobody genuinely possesses.

Only later did I understand that even this apparent normality had been won.

Walking through a city without continually inventing a false version of yourself has not always been natural. Living with another man, receiving a letter at the same address, entering a venue or being recognised without losing your job, home or family was not a gift London casually granted.

Somebody had lived openly when doing so required courage. Somebody had opened those doors. Somebody had paid first.

Today London celebrates its diversity with considerable confidence. It is right to recognise how much the city has changed.

But a city does not become kind simply because it updates the language of its advertising. It becomes kinder when it remembers the people it forced into hiding, when it protects the places where those people found one another and when it does not turn every site of memory into a luxury apartment with a discreet plaque beside the entrance, useful mainly for making the sale price appear sensitive.

Earl’s Court was my first London home. At the time, I thought it was simply a convenient, lively neighbourhood filled with temporary lives.

Now I know those streets were telling a much larger story.

“London did not become free because one day it decided to be. It became freer because somebody lived openly when doing so still came at a price.”
